## What is Decision Intelligence?

This term is chaotic right now. When you look it up, it's hard to find a consistent definition for it.

Sometimes you find jargon soup:  
> "Start with a _data_ stock. Add a dash of _machine learning_, 1 teaspoon of _AI_ from concentrate, 3-5 _visualizations_ (shelled), and 3/4 cup of diced _managerial theory_.  
> Pressure cook on high for 25 minutes. Serves 3 LinkedIn posts."

Sometimes you get vague generalizations:  
> "It's when you use AI to help make decisions!"

I think this definition [from Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/information-technology/glossary/decision-intelligence) is decent:
> "Decision intelligence (DI) is a practical discipline that advances decision making by explicitly understanding and engineering how decisions are made and how outcomes are evaluated, managed and improved via feedback."

This definition implies some follow-up questions that are a lot more specific and useful:
- How do you turn decision-making into an engineering process?
- How do you evaluate outcomes?
- What should those feedback processes look like?

One reason DI gets turned into jargon soup is because decision-making is _hard_. We've already created lots of disciplines that approach it in different ways. DI often acts as an integration framework that helps _align_ these existing tools and disciplines around common principles. So a lot of the fields attached to those tasty bits of jargon are still _involved_, but you can't just hand a data scientist an LLM and expect Decision Intelligence to materialize.

You need structure!

## Modeling decisions

Most of this post will be about that first follow-up question:
> - How do you turn decision-making into an engineering process?

The answer is _you model decisions_!

<a class="zoomable" href={Coffee_Decision_Model}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Example: Coffee Decision Model" class="img_ev3q" src={Coffee_Decision_Model}/></a>

If you can deconstruct the concept of a decision, break it down into a vocabulary of common components and features, then you can use that vocabulary to describe any decision you want.

### Why is this useful?

Once you have a common vocabulary, you can write stuff down.  
You can capture decisions! **You can _draw_ them.**

If everyone involved can see a decision laid out visually, any one of them can point at it and say "actually, I think it should look like _this_ instead".

_Conversation_ is the classic medium for decision-making. But conversation is fuzzy! Everyone has their own perspective, and it can take a lot of careful discussion to tease apart discrepancies in each person's mental model of a situation. When you model the decision instead you can collaborate, synthesizing individual mental models into a more concrete consensus. You can align around the _mechanics_ of a decision.

When your model is digital, and your vocabulary is sophisticated enough, you can even do **simulation**!

<a class="zoomable" href={CDD_Simulation}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Animated .gif of a simulated decision" class="img_ev3q" src={CDD_Simulation}/></a>
<br/><br/>
Whether you're doing realtime decision simulation or you're just collaborating on some decision diagram whiteboard doodles, DI offers enough of a framework that _engineering_ can take place. You can begin to conceptualize and discuss decisions as systems.

## Causal Decision Models

Let's dig into the specific vocabulary for those systems!

DI represents decisions visually as special [Directed Graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_graph), where elements in the directed graph are related to each other by _cause and effect_.

<a class="zoomable" href={CDD_0_Cause_Effect}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Example CDD showing cause and effect" class="img_ev3q" src={CDD_0_Cause_Effect}/></a>

When an arrow points from `Element A` to `Element B`, you can say that `Element A` _causes_ `Element B`.

Each element in a Decision Model fits into one of a few categories.
### Levers and Outcomes

These elements frame the boundaries of a decision. It often helps to brainstorm elements in these categories first when you're trying to draft a Causal Decision Model.
#### Levers

Levers represent the _actions_ available to the decision-maker.  
They answer the question "_What can I do in this situation?_" by providing some possible options.

<a class="zoomable" href={CDD_1_Lever}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Example CDD with 1 Lever" class="img_ev3q" src={CDD_1_Lever}/></a>

An example might be "Amount of money I budget for marketing this year".

#### Outcomes

Outcomes represent end results of a decision.  
This is not necessarily the same as a [Goal](https://opendi.org/OpenDI%20Glossary#goal), since Outcomes generally don't judge the success or failure of the decision.

<a class="zoomable" href={CDD_2_Outcome}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Example CDD with 1 Lever and 1 Outcome" class="img_ev3q" src={CDD_2_Outcome}/></a>

An example might be "Amount of revenue `{Company}` makes this year".

A Goal would place a _constraint_ on this Outcome, like "`{Company}` makes $`{amount}` in revenue this year".

### Intermediates

Intermediates fill in all of the causal relationships between Levers and Outcomes.  
Sometimes `Element A` does not _directly_ cause `Element B`.

<a class="zoomable" href={CDD_3_Intermediate}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Example CDD with 1 Lever, 1 Intermediate, and 1 Outcome" class="img_ev3q" src={CDD_3_Intermediate}/></a>


An example might be "Number of impressions that marketing generates this year".

Intermediates are tricky. There are often many indirect links between any given Lever and any number of Outcomes. It's common to fill these in by iterating, working inward from Levers and Outcomes.

#### Iterating with "how" and "why"

To discover _downstream_ Intermediates, ask ["Why?"](https://opendi.org/OpenDI%20Glossary/#why-chain). To discover _upstream_ Intermediates, ask ["How?"](https://opendi.org/OpenDI%20Glossary/#how-chain).

<p>
In the above example, you might ask:
> **Why** are we considering changing the marketing budget for this year?  
> _Because it will impact the number of impressions our marketing campaign generates this year._

And further, 
> **Why** is the number of impressions this year important?  
> _Because somewhere further downstream, impressions will impact our revenue this year._

From this exercise, you might conclude that it would make sense to add more intermediates to this model!

> **Why** is the number of impressions important?  
> _Because some of those impressions [convert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_marketing) into new customers, which impacts revenue._

<a class="zoomable" href={CDD_4_How_Why}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Example CDD with 1 Lever, 2 Intermediates, and 1 Outcome" class="img_ev3q" src={CDD_4_How_Why}/></a>
<br/><br/>
Going the other direction, you might ask:  
> Aside from changing the number of impressions, **how** else could we get more conversions out of our marketing budget?  
> _We might improve the quality of our marketing._

### Externals

Externals are similar to Levers. They directly affect Intermediates or Outcomes.  
Unlike Levers though, Externals are _outside_ of the decision-maker's control for the current decision.  
Externals might take on a range of possible values that the decision-maker can play with to plan for different scenarios.

<a class="zoomable" href={CDD_6_External}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Example CDD with 1 Lever, 3 Intermediates, 1 External, and 1 Outcome" class="img_ev3q" src={CDD_6_External}/></a>

An example might be "Yearly production expenses for `{Company}` goods and services".

### Keep it quantifiable

Decision elements are much more useful when they represent something explicitly _quantifiable_.

_What is the timeframe for the decision?_  
_What are the units for each Lever, Intermediate, etc.?_

Not only does quantifiability simplify simulation later on, but it also more clearly illustrates _stakes_ and _value_.  
When is this happening, and exactly how much will it help?

Our example is _mostly_ good here, but if you were bothered by the vagueness of that _Quality of marketing_ element, this might be why!

Let's unpack that a bit more.

### Decision Scope

We've identified a somewhat troublesome element here:      
> Quality of marketing this year

We've mentioned quantifiability and specificity. You might be wondering if we could make this element more meaningful. If you're asking "How?" and "Why?" you might already be thinking about the sorts of things that impact "marketing quality".

As you fill in gaps around this Intermediate, you might wonder if the quality of your marketing mostly depends on the decisions your marketing team makes when utilizing their budget. There are indeed a lot of different things they can do with that money.

This is a valid line of thinking!

If you try to model all of it, you'll likely find yourself accounting for several distinct decisions that are effectively embedded in your initial one. 

_Should the marketing team focus on quantity? What if they put more time into fewer campaigns?_

Causal decision modeling is like this! A good rule of thumb is that your model should be carefully scoped to consider only _one_ decision whenever possible. The decision-maker needs to have agency over _all_ of the relevant Levers for that one decision.

In this case, you're likely not the head of marketing, and if you were, then budget utilization is still a separate decision. So, how do you account for downstream decisions that depend on your choices, but are also ultimately outside your control? Should you model every possibility? What level of [fidelity](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/model-fidelity) is useful?

It depends!

If you're interested in accounting for a range of possibilities for budget utilization, then _"marketing budget utilization strategy"_ might become an adjustable, flat ratio of some sort (quality vs. quantity?), represented as an External.

<a class="zoomable" href={CDD_7_Scope}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Example CDD with 1 Lever, 3 Intermediates, 2 Externals, and 1 Outcome" class="img_ev3q" src={CDD_7_Scope}/></a>

This external could easily be significantly more complex!  
You could set up your ratio as a curve across a range of possible budgets. You could build a resource allocation algorithm based on prior data.  
If this becomes a big enough concern, you might realize you need to sit down with your marketing team and model this utilization decision with them, to give you a better idea of how to account for those downstream effects in this decision. If you get a solid enough model out of the exercise, you could even consider embedding it as a sub-model in a simulated version of your decision model.

### Keep it quantifiable?!

We've introduced a new External, but we haven't really fixed the vagueness of the _Quality of marketing_ Intermediate. How do you measure quality in this context? To fit it more neatly into a "quantity vs quality" dichotomy, we could measure quality in terms of _Resources devoted to each marketing campaign_.

<a class="zoomable" href={CDD_8_Quantifiability}><img decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Example CDD improved quantifiability" class="img_ev3q" src={CDD_8_Quantifiability}/></a>

This leaves us with an idea of what sorts of numbers we want to associate with this element.

## Iterate. Do the engineering!

The example model for this post has already gone through an iteration or two.

We've added a few additional Intermediates to fill out the causal links between our Levers and Outcomes. We've considered quantifiability, and thought about different options for representing more complex downstream effects. We've even noted a specific area where it could be useful to integrate prior data and talk to other experts (our marketing team).

This is engineering! We can continue to refine this.  
Later, we could hook up scripts and input/output values and actually simulate the model. We might even find a specific area or two where it makes sense to add our dash of machine learning or our AI-from-concentrate, to help model some of these causal links.

For now, we're already approaching a better understanding of this particular decision! At least in theory. I don't actually work in marketing, so hopefully I haven't misinterpreted or misapplied any basic concepts here. If I did, please [let me know](mailto:keldtdev@gmail.com)!
